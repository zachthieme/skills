# Voice Patterns

Extracted from existing writing. Update this file as more samples are added.

## Three registers, same voice

The author writes in three registers. All share the same core patterns (concrete details, problem-then-result structure, no filler). The difference is formality and warmth.

**Proposals and polished docs:** third person throughout. No "I," "we," "our," or "us." Make a named team, role, system, or "this document" the subject. No humor, declarative and thesis-driven. Use this register for proposals, strategy docs, one-pagers, and anything written for a work audience.

**Personal/blog writing:** "I" is fine, conversational asides are fine ("in true me fashion," "where's the fun in that?"), self-deprecating humor lands naturally. Use this register when the user asks for blog posts or personal writing.

**Email:** first person, warm and approachable, but clear and confident. The author leads a team of managers and writes mostly to their team, peers, and senior leadership. Readers should come away knowing what the author decided, what the author needs, and that the author has their back. Use this register for emails, replies, and chat messages. The Email section below has its specific rules; where it disagrees with a general rule, the Email section wins.

The rules below apply to both unless noted.

## Sentence structure

- 15 to 25 word declarative sentences are the default.
- Longer compound sentences (30 to 40 words) layer context before the main point. Use sparingly. In a 6-pager, cap at 30 words.
- Open with simple subject-verb: "The team ships weekly," "The question is," "The assumption is."
- Sentence fragments for emphasis, used sparingly: "Not a rewrite." "Not possible."
- Rhythm: 2 to 3 short sentences (8 to 12 words) followed by one longer explanatory sentence.

## Characteristic constructions

These are the author's recurring patterns. Use them:

- "The question is..." / "The problem is..." / "The issue is..." to frame core tensions
- "None of this is X" or "This is not inherently Y" to dismiss false equivalencies
- "The result is..." to connect scattered examples to a systemic consequence
- "What breaks is..." / "The gap is..." to isolate root causes from surface symptoms
- "must" / "must account for" / "must be" for non-negotiable requirements
- Comparative framing with "Today" as anchor: describe current state, then contrast
- "The assumption was: [statement]" to set up an approach before describing results (blog: "My assumption was:")
- "Historically, [actor] would [old way]... now [new way]" for before/after contrast (blog: "Historically, I would")
- "Anytime X is an option, [actor] leans into it" for stating preferences through action (blog: "I lean into it")

## Narrative structure

Across both registers, arguments and stories follow the same skeleton:

1. State the problem or situation concretely
2. Describe the approach taken (and why)
3. Show the tangible result with specific detail ("~10 minutes," "less than five minutes," "3 of the 5 test machines failed on first boot")

The author never describes an approach without showing what happened when it was applied. Abstract claims are always grounded in a specific outcome.

## Structure and lists

- Numbered lists only when order matters operationally (steps, procedures)
- Bullet points for parallel examples without prescribed sequence. In a 6-pager body, write these as prose; bullets only in Goals/Tenets and short metric lists.
- Sections open with 2 to 3 sentence framing paragraphs: state thesis before evidence
- Tables for dense comparative information. In a 6-pager, tables go in the appendix.
- Section headers can pose questions or name the topic directly
- Short imperative bullet lists for practical advice: "Split the config file before it passes 500 lines"

## What to avoid

- No em dashes, in any register
- No hedging: avoid "we believe," "arguably," "hopefully," "may"
- No apologetic framing
- In proposals: no first person, singular or plural. Name the actor ("the infrastructure team will migrate X," "this proposal recommends Y"). Never fall back on passive voice to avoid a pronoun.
- No vague modifiers. "30% failure rate" not "sometimes fails." "~10 minutes" not "quickly."
- Never attribute problems to vague "challenges" or "headwinds." Name the organizational gap or the specific cause.
- No rhetorical questions in body text of proposals (headers only). Blog posts can use them conversationally.

## Paragraph rhythm

- Open with a single-sentence thesis or observation
- Expand with 2 to 3 sentences of context or evidence
- Close with a definitive statement that reframes the problem or raises the stakes
- Longer paragraphs (4 to 6 sentences) for causal arguments
- Shorter paragraphs (2 to 3 sentences) for examples or objections
- Never more than 6 consecutive sentences. Break to reset reader attention.

## Bold and emphasis

- Bold is structural, not emotional
- Bold role titles, organizational terms, and category names at first mention
- Bold principles or tenets when introduced, not their explanations
- After first mention, use plain text
- Never bold for emotional emphasis or to make something sound important

## Email

The paste-ready version of this section for Outlook Copilot lives in `copilot-email-instructions.md`. Keep the two in sync.

### Structure

- Open with a brief, genuine acknowledgment when it fits ("Thanks for pulling this together, Sam."). One line.
- State the decision, direction, or ask by the second sentence. Then give the why in a sentence or two.
- When explaining a problem: what is going on, what the team is doing about it, who owns it, and what happens next.
- Bullets for 3+ action items or parallel points. Numbered lists only for steps in order.
- Most replies are 3 to 6 sentences, mostly 10 to 20 words each.

### Tone

- State decisions plainly when they are the author's to make: "Let's go with option B." "We'll hold the launch until Thursday." Never phrase them as suggestions.
- Make asks clear and friendly, with an owner and a date: "Can you send the updated numbers by Friday?" Give the reason when it isn't obvious.
- Disagree directly and respectfully: say what the author would do differently and why. Acknowledge what is good in the other person's thinking without overdoing it.
- Credit people by name and specifically ("Sam's analysis made this easy to call").
- "We" for the team, "I" for the author's own decisions and commitments. Contractions are good.
- Open the door without giving up ownership: "Happy to talk through it if you see it differently."
- With senior leadership, lead with the bottom line and the recommendation, and drop the greeting.

### Avoid in email

- Permission-seeking: "I think maybe," "if that's okay," "sorry to ask," "just wanted to," "hopefully."
- Stiff phrases: "per my last email," "please advise," "kindly," "as discussed."
- Filler: "I hope this email finds you well," "just circling back," repeated thanks or apologies.
- Bold for emphasis, and more than one exclamation point per email.

### Close

- End with the next step or a short offer to help, then "Thanks," and the author's name.
